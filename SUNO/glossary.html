BANK A

Core rap & production terms

The words that get thrown around in every session — beat, bars, flow, pocket — plus the studio shorthand that isn't genre-exclusive but is table stakes for keeping up.

Beat, bars, tempo, groove

Beat

Two meanings. In strict theory it's the recurring pulse. In rap and beatmaking, "the beat" usually means the whole instrumental: drums, bass, melodic material, transitions, arrangement.

Bars

Measures. Most rap is counted in 4/4, so one bar usually holds four quarter-note beats. "Sixteen bars" is the default verse length.

Tempo / BPM

Speed of the pulse in beats per minute.

Half-time convention added

Trap and drill are often written at 130–160 BPM but felt at half that, because the snare lands on beat 3 instead of 2 and 4. A "140 BPM" trap beat and a "70 BPM" one are frequently the same tempo counted differently — which matters when you're matching an acapella or a sample.

Groove

The feel produced by timing, accent, swing, note length, and the interaction between drums, bass, and vocal phrasing.

Pocket

A performer or part sitting rhythmically inside the groove in a way that feels locked and believable — slightly ahead pushes, slightly behind drags.

Swing

Subdivisions weighted unevenly instead of perfectly straight. Usually a percentage control in a DAW or MPC (54%–62% is the classic boom-bap zone).

Syncopation added

Accenting where the meter doesn't expect it — offbeats, "e" and "a" subdivisions, ties across the barline. It's the main engine of groove in almost all rap drum programming.

MC vocabulary

Flow

The rhythmic and prosodic design of a rap delivery — accent placement, syllable density, rhyme placement, phrase length, syncopation, and how the voice sits against the instrumental meter. Treated in rap scholarship as a real musical dimension, not vague "style."

Triplet flow

A flow habit grouping syllables in triplet-like patterns; a signature of trap-era rap.

Cadence added

The specific rhythmic shape of a phrase — how a line starts, where it accelerates, and how it lands. Rappers often have several cadences they rotate inside one verse.

Double-time / half-time added

Delivering roughly twice as many syllables per bar, or roughly half. Switching between them mid-verse is one of the cheapest ways to make a verse feel like it's building.

Multisyllabic rhyme added

Rhyming two or more syllables at once ("lateral damage / scattered advantage") rather than just the final vowel. The backbone of technical rap.

Internal rhyme added

Rhymes placed inside the line rather than only at the end, creating extra rhythmic hooks between downbeats.

Slant rhyme added

Near-rhyme built on shared vowel color or consonant shape instead of exact matching. Most modern rap rhymes are slant rhymes performed into exactness by the delivery.

Enjambment added

Letting a sentence run past the end of the bar instead of resolving at the barline. Creates forward motion and disguises predictable rhyme placement.

Entendre added

A line carrying two or more simultaneous readings. "Double entendre" is two; punchline writers stack three or four.

Punchline added

A line built for impact at its final word — usually setup, then reversal. The pause after it is part of the technique.

Breath control added

Managing where you inhale so long phrases don't collapse. In the booth it's also an editing concern — audible breaths get shaped, ducked, or cut.

Hook

The catchy musical/lyrical anchor of the song. Often the chorus, not always.

Ad-libs

Short vocal insertions around the main line that add attitude, spacing, response, and branding. Non-lexical vocables are increasingly recognized as a real component of identity and flow.

Cypher

A circle of rappers or dancers taking turns improvising or performing, often competitively.

Bite / biting

Copying another rapper's bars, cadence, concept, or distinctive style too closely.

Freestyle added

Historically "not written for a specific song"; in current usage it usually means improvised off the top. Both meanings are still in circulation and people argue about it constantly.

Genre and era tags NEW SUBSECTION

These are aesthetic families, not rigid rulesets — most modern records sit between two or three of them.

Boom-bap

Classic East Coast, sample-driven aesthetic: punchy kicks, cracked snares, dusty breaks, swing, chopped jazz/soul/funk. Snare usually on 2 and 4. The name points at both the kick/snare profile and a whole production sensibility.

Break / breakbeat

The drum-heavy section of a record, or a sampled drum passage used as a rhythmic foundation.

Trap added

808 sub-bass, sparse snare/clap on 3, hat rolls and triplet subdivisions, dark or bell-like melodic loops, heavy space.

Drill added

Sliding, portamento 808 patterns, sparse menacing melodies, distinctive hat/rimshot patterns. UK/Brooklyn drill typically sits around 138–145 BPM with a triplet-leaning skip.

Phonk added

Memphis-rooted: cowbell melodies, distorted 808s, tape-degraded samples, chopped-and-screwed vocal texture.

Plugg / rage added

Bright detuned bell and pad loops with loose, airy drum programming (plugg); or aggressive distorted synth leads over hard 808s (rage).

G-funk added

West Coast: whining portamento lead synth, slow funk grooves, Moog-style bass, live-feel percussion.

Jersey club added

Roughly 130–140 BPM, five-count kick pattern, bed-squeak sample, chopped vocal triggers. Regularly hybridized into rap records.

Cloud rap added

Washed reverb-heavy pads, slow diffuse drums, hazy vocal processing.

Arrangement and studio shorthand

Intro / verse / hook / bridge / outro

Arrangement functions. An "8-bar intro" means exactly that: eight measures before the main section arrives.

Turnaround

A short figure that resets the loop and pushes into the next section.

Drop

The point where the full groove, or a high-energy section, lands.

Beat switch added

A deliberate change of instrumental mid-song — new tempo, key, or palette. Usually placed after a hook or at the top of a final verse.

Topline

The lead vocal melody and phrasing concept written over an instrumental.

A/B section

Two contrasting arrangement blocks.

Chop

A sliced sample fragment. "Chopping" is rearranging those fragments into a new phrase.

Flip added

Recontextualizing a sample so it reads as new material — repitching, reversing, re-harmonizing, or rebuilding the groove around it.

One-shot

A single hit rather than a loop.

Loop

A repeating audio or MIDI phrase.

Resampling added

Bouncing your own processed material back to audio and treating it as a new sample. The core trick behind most "how did they get that texture" sounds.

Timestretch / repitch added

Changing length without pitch (stretch) versus changing both together like a tape machine (repitch). Repitching keeps character; stretching keeps key.

Stem

An exported subgroup — drums, bass, vocals, music.

Print

Committing an effect or instrument to audio.

Comp

Assembling the best pieces of multiple takes into one performance.

Punch-in added

Recording over a small section of an existing take instead of redoing the whole pass.

Doubles / stacks added

Re-performing a vocal line to layer with the original. Doubles thicken; stacks (many layers, often panned or harmonized) build hooks.

Bus / aux

A routing path used for subgroup processing or shared send effects like reverb and delay.

Gain staging

Managing levels through the signal chain to preserve headroom and avoid unwanted distortion or noise.

Headroom

The safety space before clipping.

Clipping

What happens when the system runs out of ceiling and the waveform gets cut off.

Sidechain

One signal controlling the processing of another — most often compression or gating.

Quantize / humanize added

Snapping notes to a grid, or nudging them off it. Quantize strength and swing amount are usually more useful than full 100% snapping.

Velocity added

How hard a MIDI note is struck. Velocity variation is what separates a programmed hat pattern from a played one.

Automation added

Recorded parameter movement over time — filter sweeps, volume rides, send throws, mute cuts.

Mixdown vs master added

Mixdown balances the individual elements into a stereo file. Mastering treats that stereo file as a finished object — final tone, loudness, and format prep.

LUFS / true peak added

Loudness measured the way streaming platforms measure it, plus the inter-sample peak ceiling that survives lossy encoding. Most platforms normalize toward roughly −14 LUFS, so mastering to −6 LUFS mostly buys you a turn-down, not more volume.

Reference track added

A finished commercial record used as a tonal and loudness benchmark while mixing. Level-matched, or it's useless.

Business shorthand that shows up in sessions NEW SUBSECTION

Type beat added

A beat marketed as being in the style of a named artist. A distribution category more than a musical one.

Lease vs exclusive added

A lease licenses non-exclusive use, usually with caps; an exclusive transfers the right to that beat to one buyer under agreed terms.

Split sheet added

The document recording who wrote what percentage, signed in the room while everyone still agrees.

Clearance vs interpolation added

Clearing means licensing the actual recording and the composition. Interpolation means re-performing the melody instead of using the recording — which still requires the publishing side, just not the master.

Producer tag added

A short branded audio signature dropped at the top of the beat.

BANK B

Sound palette, drums & instruments

Everything that occupies space in the arrangement: the atmosphere underneath, the transitions between, the drums that carry it, and the instrument families that color it.

Atmosphere, ear candy, and transitions

Ambient sounds

Environmental or background layers suggesting a space, weather, location, or emotional atmosphere. In rap they're usually subtle: room tone, vinyl wash, city ambience, tape hiss, drones, filtered crowd beds, rain, traffic, radio bleed, abstract pads.

Atmospheric noise

Broader term for non-melodic background material adding depth, tension, or mood.

Ear candy

Small, often momentary decorative details that aren't structurally necessary but make a production feel finished and replayable: filtered vocal tosses, reverse textures, glitches, impact tails, panned fills, ghost harmonies, short synth licks, micro-delays.

White noise

Equal intensity across the audible spectrum; perceptually a hiss. Used for cymbal-like material, snare layers, risers, sweeps, tension beds, and transitional FX — usually filtered and automated rather than raw.

Pink / brown noise added

Noise weighted toward the low end (pink falls 3 dB per octave, brown 6 dB). Pink reads warmer and more natural than white; brown reads like rumble or wind and works well under drops.

Impact

A short, forceful accent marking an arrival, cut point, drop-in, or dramatic hit.

Whoosh

A rushing motion effect implying speed, transition, movement, and entry.

Riser / downlifter added

Ascending tension build into a section, or descending release out of one. Together with impacts and whooshes they're the actual backbone of section transitions, even when the harmony is sparse.

Drone added

A sustained, usually low, unchanging tone under the arrangement. Adds continuity and dread cheaply.

Tape stop added

Pitch and speed collapsing to a halt, imitating a stopped tape reel. A transition cliché that still works.

Reverse cymbal / reverse tail added

Reversed audio swelling into a downbeat. Cheapest riser you already own.

Drop-out added

Deliberately removing elements — often everything but vocal — right before a return. Silence is a transition effect.

Foley added

Recorded real-world sound used musically: lighter flicks, chains, door slams, coins, camera shutters. Common as percussion substitutes in trap and drill.

Groove archetypes

Four-on-the-floor

A steady kick on all four quarter-note beats of a 4/4 bar. Foundational in disco and house; borrowed into club rap, Jersey-influenced records, drill crossovers, and pop-rap.

Backbeat

The accented beats that create the familiar crack in common time — usually 2 and 4. In boom-bap the snare or clap defines it.

Half-time feel added

Snare on beat 3 only, which halves the perceived tempo while the hats keep the actual grid. The defining feel of trap.

Bossa nova / samba

Brazilian rhythmic traditions built on syncopation and sway. In hip-hop the reference usually means borrowed chord-guitar patterns, syncopated percussion, brushed drums, nylon-string texture, or jazz/Brazilian harmonic color — not literal genre conversion.

Dembow added

The reggaeton/dancehall kick–snare cell (often written "boom-ch-boom-chick"). Increasingly common under Latin-leaning rap.

Log drum added

The pitched, sliding, hollow bass hit from amapiano — functionally an 808 with a percussive envelope and glide baked in.

Drums and percussion

Kick

The low drum anchoring pulse and physical impact. Can be acoustic, sampled, synthesized, layered, or split into transient / body / sub components.

Snare

Snap and backbeat.

Clap

A handclap-style transient, frequently layered with the snare.

Rimshot / side-stick

Sharper or woodier stick sounds.

Hi-hat

Paired cymbals played closed, open, or with the pedal. Closed hats are short and tight; open hats ring longer and feel more expansive.

Electronic vs acoustic hats

Sample/synthesis-based hats from drum machines are brighter, more controllable, more stylized. Recorded cymbals bring physical variation and complex overtones.

Hat roll added

A burst of fast repeated hats — 1/16, 1/32, triplet, or accelerating. Velocity and slight pitch variation are what stop it sounding like a machine gun.

Choke added

Cutting an open hat or cymbal short with the next hit, usually via a mute/choke group. Small detail, big groove difference.

Ghost note added

A very quiet hit between the main ones. Fills the grid without adding perceived density.

Perc loop added

A layered percussion pattern — shakers, congas, bongos, tambourine, rims — running under the main kit to add motion.

808 / 909 / 707 added

Roland drum machines whose sample sets became genre-defining. The 808 anchored hip-hop, the 909 anchored house and techno; both are now sound categories more than machines.

Layering added

Stacking two or three sounds to build one hit — transient from one, body from another, tail from a third. Check phase; two kicks summed wrong get quieter, not bigger.

808 bass

808 bass

Originally the bass drum of the Roland TR-808. In modern rap it means a tuned, sustained low-end sound derived from or inspired by that kick — pitched melodically, distorted, saturated, layered with a kick, or glided. A modern 808 isn't "a kick"; it's the sub-bass instrument of the track.

Glide / portamento added

Sliding continuously between 808 notes instead of retriggering. The defining gesture of drill basslines; controlled by glide time and overlapping notes in the piano roll.

Tuning the 808 added

Setting the 808's root to the key of the track. An untuned 808 fights the melody harmonically even when the pattern is right.

Sub vs harmonics added

The fundamental lives below roughly 60 Hz where phones and laptops can't reproduce it. Distortion or saturation generates upper harmonics so the ear infers the missing fundamental — which is why heavy 808s translate and clean ones vanish on small speakers.

Kick/808 relationship added

Three standard approaches: sidechain the 808 to the kick, place them so they never overlap, or high-pass the kick and let the 808 own everything below it.

Instrument families

Synth

Electronically generated and modified sound. In rap that spans pads, keys, leads, plucks, bells, Reese basses, and pure texture.

Melody

The line listeners perceive as the tune or lead contour.

Bassline

The low-pitched line supporting groove and harmony.

Chord

Multiple notes sounding together as a harmonic unit.

Brass

Lip-vibrated winds — trumpet, trombone, horn, tuba. In hip-hop they usually signal power, triumph, tension, or sample-era richness.

Woodwind

Flute and reed instruments — clarinet, oboe, bassoon, saxophone. In rap they read airy, jazzy, eerie, soulful, or cinematic depending on arrangement and processing.

Keys family added

Rhodes and Wurlitzer (warm, bell-like, neo-soul staple), Clavinet (funk bite), Hammond organ with Leslie, upright and felt piano, celesta and mallets.

Plucks and bells added

Short-decay pitched sounds carrying trap and plugg melodies — kalimba, marimba, music box, FM bells, detuned saw plucks.

Pads added

Slow-attack sustained harmonic beds. Often the only thing holding the harmony together once the melody is chopped.

Vocal chops added

Sliced vocal fragments played melodically as an instrument. Formant shifting keeps them from sounding like chipmunks when pitched far.

Strings added

Sustained sections for weight, staccato/pizzicato for rhythm, solo violin or cello for intimacy. Layered strings are the fastest way to make a sparse beat feel expensive.

BANK C

Pitch, scales, modes & harmony

What people actually mean when they say a beat is "in a key," why the note name isn't the feeling, and the chord vocabulary that separates a two-finger loop from a record.

Read this before the scale list Specific note names do not carry fixed emotions in modern equal temperament. C, D, E and the rest are pitch classes. The feeling listeners perceive comes from mode, harmony, scale-degree function, register, tempo, timbre, articulation, and cultural context — not the letter. Major/minor differences are well documented in perception research and modal color is widely taught, but "C sounds like X forever" is not a law of music. What producers call the "vibe of a note" is usually one of four other things: the key center, the scale-degree function, the register, or the ergonomics of that instrument in that key.

Practical color tendencies

Not rules. Working shorthand that holds up often enough to be useful.

Major

Brighter, more open on average.

Minor

Darker, more introspective on average.

Lydian

Dreamy, lifted — the raised fourth.

Phrygian

Tense, dark — the lowered second.

Dorian

Minor, but more hopeful and mobile than natural minor — the raised sixth.

Mixolydian

Major, but earthier and bluesier — the lowered seventh.

Aeolian / Locrian added

Aeolian is natural minor. Locrian, with its lowered second and lowered fifth, has no stable tonic triad and is rarely used as a key center — mostly a passing color.

Scales and modes

Major scale

The familiar seven-note diatonic collection — bright, stable, open.

Minor scale

Usually natural minor in casual producer talk, though harmonic and melodic minor are also in play.

Mode

A scale derived from a parent collection but with a different tonal center and interval structure. The diatonic modes: Ionian (major), Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian (natural minor), Locrian.

Pentatonic

A five-note collection. Common in pop, rock, blues, trap melodies, drill, and hooks because it's singable, memorable, and avoids some friction points of denser scales. Major and minor pentatonic are the workhorses.

Whole-tone

Six notes, all whole steps. Floating, ambiguous, dreamy, uncanny — it lacks the leading-tone gravity of diatonic harmony.

Octatonic

Eight notes alternating semitone and whole tone. Tense, unstable, cinematic, symmetrical — useful for dark, modern, or horror-coded color.

Pelog

A heptatonic tuning/scale family from Javanese gamelan with unequal intervals, usually realized in performance through five-note subsets. In beatmaking, "pelog-ish" means borrowing that uneven, non-major/non-minor gamelan-adjacent flavor rather than reproducing traditional practice.

Harmonic minor added

Natural minor with a raised seventh, creating the augmented-second leap between ♭6 and ♮7. Reads dramatic, "Eastern," or classical-menacing.

Melodic minor added

Raised sixth and seventh ascending. Traditionally reverts descending; in jazz and modern production it's usually treated as a fixed collection.

Blues scale added

Minor pentatonic plus the ♭5 "blue note." Bends and microtonal inflection matter more than the written pitches.

Phrygian dominant added

Fifth mode of harmonic minor — lowered second over a major third. A staple of dark trap and drill melodies.

Double harmonic / Hungarian minor added

Two augmented seconds in one scale. Extremely characterful, easy to overuse.

Hirajoshi / in scale added

Japanese pentatonic families with distinctive semitone placement. Frequently borrowed for koto- and kalimba-style loops — worth learning by ear rather than by formula.

Chromaticism added

Using notes outside the key as passing or coloring tones. One chromatic approach note into a target does more for a flat melody than a whole new scale.

Chords, voicings, inversions

Diatonic triads

Three-note chords built from a scale by stacking thirds; the basic harmonic palette of a key.

Seventh chords

Another third on top, producing maj7, min7, dom7, half-diminished, and diminished-seventh colors. Crucial in R&B, jazz rap, neo-soul, and sample-flip production, where plain triads read as thin.

Voicing

How chord tones are arranged in register and spacing. The same chord can sound dense, open, glossy, crunchy, intimate, or cinematic depending on voicing alone.

Inversion

A chord tone other than the root in the bass. First inversion = third in bass; second = fifth; seventh chords add a third inversion with the seventh in the bass. Inversions smooth voice-leading, change bass movement, and shift emotional weight without changing the chord label.

Extensions (9, 11, 13) added

Notes stacked past the seventh. The 9th adds shimmer, the ♯11 adds lift, the 13th adds warmth. In practice you usually drop the fifth to make room.

Sus2 / sus4 added

The third replaced by a second or fourth, leaving the chord neither major nor minor. Perfect for loops that need to stay ambiguous under a vocal.

Add9 / 6/9 added

Triads with an added ninth or added sixth-and-ninth, keeping the third but adding color without the weight of a full seventh chord.

Slash chord added

Written as chord/bass note, e.g. Am/G. In beats it's how you get a descending bassline under a static chord loop.

Quartal voicing added

Stacking fourths instead of thirds. Open, modern, ambiguous — the McCoy Tyner / neo-soul keyboard sound.

Drop 2 added

Taking the second-highest note of a close voicing and dropping it an octave. The default way to make four-note chords sit well on keys or guitar.

Voice leading added

Moving individual chord tones the shortest distance between chords. The single highest-leverage skill for making a loop sound written rather than clicked in.

Modal interchange added

Borrowing chords from the parallel major or minor — ♭VI, ♭VII, iv in a major key. Where most "how did that chord get there" moments come from.

Secondary dominant added

A dominant chord aimed at a scale degree other than the tonic (V/V, V/vi). Adds pull inside an otherwise flat progression.

Tritone substitution added

Replacing a dominant with the dominant a tritone away, producing chromatic bass motion into the target.

Pedal point added

Holding one bass note while harmony moves above it. Extremely common in rap, where the 808 sits on the tonic through a whole loop.

Loop harmony vs functional harmony added

Most rap harmony is a two-to-four-chord loop with no cadence and no resolution — the interest comes from rhythm, timbre, and vocal placement. Applying classical cadence logic to a beat loop usually just makes it stop feeling like a loop.

Bass and register

Bassline (in practice)

The low-register line outlining roots, counterlines, glides, pedal points, or rhythmic motion. In rap it's often minimalist but essential: sustained 808s, syncopated sub phrases, octave jumps, slide notes, pedal drones, call-and-response against the kick. When a producer says a beat "has no movement," they usually mean the bass–drum relationship is too static.

Register language added

Rough working bands: sub below ~60 Hz (felt), low 60–200 Hz (weight), low-mid 200–500 Hz (mud/body), mid 500 Hz–2 kHz (definition), presence 2–6 kHz (intelligibility, harshness), air above 8 kHz (sheen).

Equal temperament / A440 added

The standard modern tuning system dividing the octave into twelve equal semitones, referenced to A = 440 Hz. It's the reason no key is acoustically "special" anymore — the pitch-class caveat at the top of this section follows directly from it.

BANK D

Effects & processing

Dynamics, spectrum, time, modulation, saturation, pitch, and the vocal chain — grouped by what they actually manipulate rather than by plugin folder.

Dynamics

Compression

Reduces level above a threshold, narrowing dynamic range and often raising average loudness, consistency, and punch. Core controls: threshold, ratio, attack, release, knee, makeup gain. Slower attack lets a transient through; faster attack clamps it.

Limiter

Effectively a compressor with a very high or infinite ratio, used to stop peaks exceeding a ceiling.

Expander

The opposite of compression — increases dynamic contrast, often making quiet material quieter.

Gate

Extreme downward expansion that attenuates or mutes signal below a threshold.

De-esser

A sidechain-sensitive compressor specialized for reducing sibilance.

Transient shaper

A dynamics processor targeting attack and sustain specifically, rather than overall level.

Multiband compression added

Compression applied independently per frequency band, so a loud low end doesn't duck the whole track.

Dynamic EQ added

An EQ band that only moves when the signal crosses a threshold. Better than static EQ for problems that come and go — an occasional harsh consonant, a resonant note in a bass part.

Glue compression added

Gentle bus compression (low ratio, slow attack, program-dependent release) to make separate elements read as one performance.

Clipper vs limiter added

A limiter turns peaks down; a clipper cuts their tops off. Soft clipping an 808 or a drum bus before the limiter buys loudness without the pumping a limiter would introduce.

Parallel compression

Blending a heavily compressed copy under the dry signal — density without fully killing transients.

In practice, for rap Compression for vocal consistency · parallel compression for density · sidechain compression to duck bass under kick · gates and expanders to clean noisy recordings and tighten room spill · de-essing to smooth harsh consonants · transient shaping to add punch to kicks, snares, and percussion without the collateral damage of heavy compression.

EQ, filters, and stereo

EQ

Changes the balance of frequency ranges — carving mud, adding presence, brightening hats, controlling harshness, shaping reverb tails.

Filters

High-pass removes lows, low-pass removes highs, band-pass isolates a region, notch attenuates a narrow trouble spot.

Panning

Position in the stereo field; width controls change how mono or wide the image reads.

Spectral effects

A loose category in casual producer talk — tools that shape tone or frequency-space rather than time or dynamics.

Mid/side processing added

Treating the centre and the sides of a stereo image separately. Lets you widen highs while keeping bass and vocal locked in the middle.

Mono bass added

Collapsing everything below roughly 100–150 Hz to mono. Prevents phase cancellation on club systems and vinyl.

Haas widening added

A delay under ~30 ms on one side reads as width rather than echo. Cheap and effective — and the first thing to break in mono, so check.

Phase / polarity added

Polarity flips the waveform; phase relates to timing offset. Layered drums and multi-mic recordings lose low end when these fight.

Resonance / Q added

How narrow an EQ band or how emphasized a filter cutoff is. High resonance on a sweeping filter is a musical effect in itself.

Time-based effects

Reverb

Simulates the persistence of reflected sound in a space. Algorithmic or convolution-based; can create rooms, halls, plates, shimmer tails, or deliberately artificial textures.

Analog / mechanical reverb

Classic physical devices — springs, plates — or digital emulations of them.

Delay

Repeats audio after a set time. Short delays thicken; long synced delays create rhythmic call-and-response.

Echo

Often a natural-language synonym for delay, though people sometimes reserve it for more obvious, spacious repeats. Filtered feedback delays are a staple of dub, trap transitions, ad-lib throws, and psychedelic rap texture.

Pre-delay added

The gap before the reverb starts. 20–60 ms keeps a vocal intelligible while still placing it in a big space.

Decay / size / damping added

How long the tail lasts, how large the simulated space is, and how quickly high frequencies die inside it. Damping is what stops a big reverb sounding like a bathroom.

Ducked reverb added

Sidechaining the reverb return to the dry vocal so the tail only blooms in the gaps. How modern rap vocals stay dry-sounding and huge at once.

Gated reverb added

A reverb tail cut short by a gate — abrupt, dramatic, unmistakably eighties on snares, but useful on any hit that needs size without smear.

Reverse reverb added

Reversed tail swelling into the source. Classic pre-vocal entrance move.

Throw added

Automating a send so one word or ad-lib gets delayed or drenched while the rest stays dry.

Slapback / ping-pong / tape delay added

Single short repeat (slapback), repeats alternating between speakers (ping-pong), or repeats that degrade and drift in pitch (tape). Dotted-1/8 sync is the default rhythmic delay setting for a reason.

Modulation

Chorus

Duplicates and slightly delays/modulates a signal so one source feels like several.

Flanger

Mixes the signal with a very short modulated delay, producing sweeping comb-filter motion.

Phaser

Uses phase-shifting filters rather than delay lines for notchy movement.

Tremolo

Modulates amplitude — pulsing volume.

Vibrato

Modulates pitch rather than level.

Rotary

Simulates the motion and Doppler-like character of a spinning speaker cabinet.

Auto-pan added

LFO-driven stereo movement. Tempo-synced, it becomes a rhythmic element rather than an effect.

Ring modulation added

Multiplies two signals, producing sum and difference frequencies. Inharmonic, metallic, robotic — great for FX, rough on melodies.

LFO added

Low-frequency oscillator: the slow waveform driving most modulation. Rate, depth, shape, and sync are the four controls that matter.

Saturation and distortion

Saturation

Distortion historically associated with overdriving analog components — tubes, transformers, tape. Adds harmonics, mild compression, density, and glue.

Tape saturation

Tape-like nonlinearity, softening, and compression behavior.

Valve / tube saturation

Same idea, different vocabulary — "valve" is the British term for tube.

Overdrive

Warmer, smoother, less aggressive distortion.

Distortion

The broad category for stronger nonlinear alteration.

Fuzz

Extreme, buzzy, broken, smeared distortion texture.

Clipping (digital vs analog)

Waveform truncation past the available ceiling. Digital clipping is hard and abrupt; analog-style clipping transitions more softly.

Bitcrusher

Reduces bit depth and/or sample rate for gritty, aliased, vintage-digital or aggressively degraded sound.

Harmonic exciter added

Generates upper harmonics to add perceived brightness without simply boosting EQ. Easy to overdo on vocals.

Wavefolding added

Folds the waveform back on itself past a threshold, producing complex inharmonic content. West Coast synthesis staple, increasingly used on 808s.

Pitch and vocal effects

Transpose

Moving notes up or down by a set interval.

Pitch shifter

Changes pitch of audio after recording or in real time.

Harmonizer

Generates added pitched voices above or below the original, by interval or by key.

Vocoder

Imposes the spectral/rhythmic character of one signal — typically speech — onto the harmonic body of another, typically a synth or pad. The "talking synth" sound.

Talkbox

A related vocalized effect achieved physically: sound is fed into the performer's mouth through a tube, the mouth shapes the harmonics, and a mic captures the result.

Hard tune added

Pitch correction with retune speed set fast enough that transitions between notes become audible steps. Retune speed and the chosen scale are the two settings that define the sound.

Formant shifting added

Moving the resonant character of a voice independently of its pitch. Keeps heavily pitched vocals from sounding like chipmunks, or deliberately makes them monstrous.

Doubler added

Generates artificial doubles with small pitch and timing offsets. Not a replacement for a real double, but useful on ad-libs.

Telephone / lo-fi EQ added

Aggressive band-pass plus distortion, imitating a phone line or radio. Standard for intros and contrast sections.

Utility terms worth knowing NEW SUBSECTION

Dry / wet added

The balance between unprocessed and processed signal. On a send, keep the effect 100% wet and control the blend with the send level.

Insert vs send added

An insert processes the whole channel; a send copies signal to a shared bus. Reverb and delay usually belong on sends so several sources share one space.

Oversampling added

Running a nonlinear process at a higher internal sample rate to reduce aliasing. Matters most on distortion, clippers, and limiters.

Latency / PDC added

Processing delay, and the DAW's plugin delay compensation that realigns it. Look-ahead limiters and linear-phase EQs are the usual culprits when tracking feels laggy.

Dither added

Low-level noise added when reducing bit depth, to avoid quantization distortion. Applied once, at the very last export.

Beatbox & vocal sounds

Different rules apply here. Many of these names are community labels, onomatopoeic aliases, or performer-branded techniques. Some are stable scene-wide; others vary by teacher, region, or a single Discord.

Standard stable across the scene
Community widely used, definition drifts

Two housekeeping notes Where a description came from your own notes, it's preserved in cleaned-up glossary form. Where naming is obscure or unstable, the entry says so rather than inventing one universal definition. And a spelling fix: "poluphonic voice" is almost certainly polyphonic voice.

Airflow and mechanics NEW SUBSECTION

The physical vocabulary underneath almost every named sound below. Learning these makes unfamiliar technique names decodable instead of memorizable.

Outward vs inward Standard added

Whether the sound is made pushing air out or drawing it in. Inward sounds are how beatboxers sustain long passages — you're inhaling while still producing rhythm, which is why inward bass and inward snares dominate battle routines.

Airflow economy Community added

Structuring a pattern so outward and inward sounds alternate, letting you breathe inside the beat rather than between phrases.

Place of articulation Standard added

Where in the mouth the sound is formed — bilabial (both lips), labiodental (lip to teeth), alveolar (tongue to ridge behind teeth), velar (back of tongue to soft palate), uvular, glottal. Nearly all snare and hat variants are just articulation swaps.

Plosive / fricative / ejective Standard added

Pressure released suddenly (plosive — kicks, K snares), air forced through a narrow gap (fricative — hats, brushes), or pressure generated by the closed glottis rather than the lungs (ejective — the sharp, dry, "sample-like" hits).

False vocal folds Standard added

The structures above the true vocal cords, vibrated to produce growl, throat bass, and distortion. Should feel effortful in the throat but never painful — pain means you're using the true folds and should stop.

Resonance / cavity shaping Standard added

Changing the size and shape of the mouth cavity to filter the same source into different timbres. This is why "oo," "ee," and "ah" positions turn one throat sound into three.

Vocal health Standard added

Hydration, warm-up, and stopping at the first sign of pain or hoarseness. Throat bass and heavy inward work are the techniques most likely to injure people who push through discomfort.

Notation and the classic pattern NEW SUBSECTION

Written notation Community added

The scene's informal shorthand: b = kick, t or ts = closed hi-hat, tss = open hat, pf = lip-oscillation snare, k = K snare. Not standardized, but readable almost anywhere.

"Boots and cats" Community added

The teaching mnemonic for the basic pattern — b t k t — where the words train the mouth into kick, hat, snare, hat before you know the names.

Foundational percussion sounds

Kick drum Standard

The basic bass-drum imitation and usually the first foundational sound learned. "Kick" also names a whole family of kick techniques.

Closed / open hi-hat Standard added

Short t/ts fricative versus the sustained tss. Together with the kick and snare these are the three sounds every routine is built on.

PF snare Standard added

The classic outward snare made by a sharp lip release. The first snare most people learn and still the most versatile.

K snare Standard

A snare-like hit built around a sharp /k/ release and mouth pressure.

Inward K snare Standard added

The K snare performed on an inward airstream — sharper, drier, and the standard way to keep snares going through long inward passages.

Couch snare Community

Usually a softer, padded, dry snare with a cushioned attack rather than a papery crack. Exact technique varies by teacher.

Dry kick Community

A kick with minimal resonance or lip body — short, tight, sample-like.

Rimshot Standard

A snare-side-stick style click or crack; in beatbox usually a small woody accent that cuts more than it booms.

Click roll Standard

A sustained roll from repeating inward or lateral tongue clicks. Useful for fills, bass accompaniment, or aggressive texture.

Sucker punch Community

A punchy burst-snare or kick-snare hybrid emphasizing sudden pressure release and attack. Community slang, not standardized pedagogy.

Inward bass pitched PF snare Community

A compound effect — a high-pitched inward snare burst layered against or around a breathless PF-style punch snare, used to vary sustained inward bass passages.

Kenny Urban fart snare Unverified

From your notes: an elongated BMG-style snare shaped using air from the top lip rather than a neutral push.

Spit / cough snare Community added

Aggressive snare variants driven by a forceful glottal or expulsive burst. Loud, dry, and hard on the throat if overused.

Basses and rolls

Lip bass Standard

A bass timbre from lip vibration rather than throat-fold distortion — usually buzzier and more mid-forward than deep throat basses.

Lip roll Standard

One of the central modern bass sounds: loose lip oscillation driven by air pressure, often with a side preference and many variants.

Reverse lip roll Community

The lip-roll concept inverted in airflow direction or shaping. Community definitions vary.

Outward sub bass Standard

A low bass on outward airflow with low-frequency emphasis — usually less distorted than inward bass and more pressure-dependent.

Inward bass Standard

The powerful inward-airflow bass family that dominates modern battle beatbox. Can be sustained and pitched, and sits between growl and synth-bass territory.

Throat bass Standard

Low, distorted, throat-driven bass — broader and rougher than clean voiced singing, with more chest and throat engagement.

OD bass Community

"Overdrive" bass: saturated and aggressive, built from compressed outward or inward throat/lip resonance.

Villain OD bass Unverified

From your notes: OD bass performed on the outward lip, with an inward variant resembling an inward click roll on the lips.

Fart bass Community

A deliberately raspy, loose, lip-driven bass whose timbre resembles a rude noise rather than a clean sub.

Snore bass Community

A bass built from snore-like oral/throat turbulence; can be mixed with hum or inward percussion.

Kenôzen grumble snore bass Unverified

From your notes: a collapsing pressure-column outward snore/grumble effect that can be layered with inward kick and humming for covers.

Hollow clop Community

A cavity-heavy clopping bass/percussive hit with hollow resonance.

D-Low bubble roll Unverified

From your notes: a side-positioned inward lip-roll setup where the lip flaps back and forth rather than vibrating like a standard lip roll. Related to lip-roll mechanics but distinct in feel.

Slizzer roll Unverified

Community roll/bass alias, usually a liquid, zipping, side-oriented lip/tongue roll family. Definition varies by teacher.

Snore vs throat vs inward Standard

The easiest distinction is source: snore bass emphasizes snore-like turbulence, throat bass emphasizes throat distortion, inward bass emphasizes inward airflow and modern battle sustain.

Tongue bass Community added

A bass produced by tongue oscillation against the palate or teeth rather than the lips or folds. Sits lower in effort than throat bass, which makes it useful for long passages.

Pitched bass Standard added

Any bass technique controlled melodically by adding hum or shifting cavity size — the step between "having a bass" and playing basslines.

Whistles, pitched, and polyphonic sounds

Polyphonic voice Standard

Producing more than one apparent pitch or pitch-stream at once — via overtone emphasis, false-fold interaction, or a split between a sung fundamental and shaped harmonics.

Overtone singing Standard

Harmonic filtering that makes upper overtones salient enough to read as simultaneous pitched lines.

Whistle register Standard

The extreme upper vocal register above ordinary falsetto and head voice — the Mariah Carey reference. A vocal-register term, not a beatbox sound.

Cricket whistle / Beatfox chirp Unverified

From your notes: a tooth-whistle setup with the front teeth deep into the lower lip, plus tongue roll or uvula vibration, producing a chirping insect-like whistle.

Throat whistle Unverified

An obscure whistle effect associated in your notes with Trung Bao. Not a standardized published term.

Double pucker whistle Unverified

From your notes: a pucker whistle split into two openings to produce two vibrations, potentially two simultaneous notes.

Shah bird whistle Unverified

The "National Geographic effect" — an outward bird whistle layered with humming or another simultaneous voicing. Exact physiology unclear.

Electric guitar chord Unverified

From your notes: falsetto inward bass with the mouth in a siren-like resonance position, producing a crunchy harmonized chordal effect.

Bubble synth Community

From your notes: a throat kick combined with a siren or "oo" resonance cavity, producing a rounded electronic bubble-like timbre.

Show-Go bubble synth technique Unverified

A sequence-building version of the above, shaping throat-kick bursts with consonants.

Uvula synth Unverified

From your notes: a head-voice burst combined with uvula oscillation.

Inward siren Standard

The familiar siren contour performed on an inward airstream.

Clown horn Community

Rapid alternation between outward and inward siren directions, imitating a balloon-squeeze horn.

Humming layer Standard added

Sustaining a sung pitch underneath percussive sounds so a routine carries melody and rhythm at once. The foundation of most "cover"-style beatboxing.

The main "bass engine" families in advanced beatbox today, with heavy overlap across battle routines and cover-style performance.

Performance and battle vocabulary NEW SUBSECTION

Mic technique Standard added

Cupping the mic to seal and boost low end, controlling distance for proximity effect, angling off-axis for plosives, and managing your own gain by backing off on loud sounds. Half the sound of a battle set is mic control.

Routine Standard added

A structured, rehearsed sequence built around drops, switches, and a payoff — the unit of performance in showcases and battles.

Battle formats Standard added

Showcase (solo set for judges), elimination rounds, wildcard (a submitted video that earns entry), tag team, crew, and loopstation as a separate category.

Loopstation terms Standard added

Track, overdub, undo, one-shot pad, and the discipline of committing — a layer recorded slightly out of time is stuck there for the whole set. The Boss RC-505 is the scene-standard unit.

Sound layering Community added

Producing two sounds simultaneously — hum under percussion, bass under hats, whistle over throat. The main thing separating advanced from intermediate performers.

Sound design recipes

Reverse 808 inhale

Reverse a short 808 and fade it in right before the real 808 hits. Makes the drop feel like it's sucking the air out of the room.

Gun-cocking rhythm, no cliché

Build percussion from metallic clicks, slide racks, revolver spins, and chamber snaps instead of gunshot samples. Same tension, none of the wear.

Luxury menace ambience bed

Stack low: casino floor murmur, elevator ding, ice in a whiskey glass, leather seat creak, distant siren, rain on a windshield, money-counter machine.

Reverse reverb entrance

Take any one-shot — koto, bassoon, duduk, piano, vocal ad-lib — add reverb, print it, reverse it, and place it directly before the dry hit. Instant cinematic villain entrance.

Broken music box in the trap house

Simple minor melody on music box, detune slightly, add vinyl wobble, layer an 808 underneath.

Credits & limits

Where this vocabulary comes from, and which parts of it you should not trust as fixed.

Sources for the standardized half

The production, engineering, synthesis, and theory entries lean on educational and manufacturer documentation — the kind published by DAW and plugin makers (Ableton, iZotope, Native Instruments, Apple) and by music-school programs. Those definitions are reasonably stable and you can look them up.

The beatbox tutorial lineage

Channels and artists named in your notes: TylaDubya · Kevin316 · The Orthobox · HUMAN BEATBOX · 80Fitz · Tom Thum · RUBIK · Cirby Beatbox · Lemons · MrWobbles · Nemesis Beatbox · Sid Fx · Sevan Beatbox · PingPong Productions · Deepak Ravichandran · FGGT · Mika Vilpas · Swissbeatbox · Beatbox International · Beatbox Fight Zone · BEATBOX BATTLE · Alem · MB14 · Dharni · Trung Bao. These are valuable practical learning references — they are not terminological standards bodies. In beatbox culture names drift, overlap, and mutate as techniques spread.

Lowest-confidence entries telemetry · valoop · contrix babeli combo · pash shimmerl, some performer-tagged scratches, and certain whistle and throat sounds whose names are community shorthand rather than formal categories. For those, this glossary gives the safest high-confidence reading — what kind of sound and function the term usually points at — rather than a false claim of one official anatomy.

How to pin down an unstable term NEW SUBSECTION

Find the origin video added

Most performer-named sounds trace to one tutorial or one battle set. The earliest demonstration is the definition; everything after it is interpretation.

Describe the mechanism, not the name added

"Inward, lips, side of the mouth, flapping rather than vibrating" survives across communities. "Bubble roll" does not.

Ask what slot it fills added

Kick, snare, hat, bass, lead, or FX. Function is stable even when naming isn't — which is what the translation table in Bank E is for.

Log your own variants added

If you develop a sound that doesn't match any published name, write down the airflow, articulation point, and cavity shape. That note will outlast whatever nickname it picks up.
